#### 1. Make sure you set the right criteria

It is incredibly important to know what you need from someone before you hire them. As someone who works in technology, you know this better than anyone. To make sure you're not wasting anybody's time, you need a list of technologies to be able to match profiles with. You want to make sure you have at least 15 items on your list. Don't worry if your team hasn't even heard of some of the items you include, it is important to have a strict set of criteria, how else you want to filter out thousands of desperate applicants?

In case you're struggling to complete your list, here is a bulletproof sample of the must haves for a Senior Frontend Developer:

#### 2. Attract the right candidates

You have a rought idea of your requirements, time to lay out what you can offer. Programmers are hard to satisfy. Since they spend most of their time looking at screens, they are detatched from reality, so it is your job to make them feel connected by providing an office space, a nespresso machine and a ping pong table, planning a routine of daily team meetings, .

**Pro tip**: offer a free Headspace subscription, so when you decide to dump the project they are going to work for next year because it's actually not what you wanted to begin with, they won't be able to complain about mental health.

#### 3. Plan the process

Hiring is hard, so you have to make sure you invest the effort and the time. In order to be able to pick the best candidate, you have to make sure your process is bulletproof. You have to have at least one take home test, a few technical interview rounds, algorithm solving, live coding, an architecture and design session, HR assessment, personality and IQ test, psychological assesment, previous personal and work references, and you should definitely check their horoscope compatibility. This way you can not only test their abilities but also perseverance and commitment to pursue a job at your company, and make sure they don't just take the easy route and accept another offer from someone else less demanding. This is a battle, and the numbers are against you. If you wear your enemy down, you can win the war. You can take their desperation and use it against them. Don't forget: there is nothing you can do wrong, you are the one who holds the cards.

#### 4. Prepare for the interview

This part is very easy, it consists of one simple step: have their CV open before starting the interview. This will be your questionnaire, your cheat sheet. All you have to do is to pick out random facts, and ask them so you can see if they remember. For example, you can ask: "What is your name?". If they answer "John, nice to meet you!", but the CV says "Mariana Walker", they are obviously either trying to fool you, or they simply don't remember. Either way, this is a huge red flag. It's important to keep your confidence while you decide how to move forward. Here you can cut the interview short, or see if they can remember anything else, collect all their mistakes onto a sheet, take a screenshot and post it to Linkedin before rejecting them for real.

#### 5. Present yourself

At this stage you must have hundreds of applicants in the pipeline, and dozens of interviews scheduled. Presenting yourself over and over is not easy, often tiring, annoying, and time consuming. It is best not to try and hide your emotions, this will not only position you as someone honest and relatable, but it will set the tone for the interview, and give you the upper hand. You don't want to hire anyone weak who is that easily triggered, so it is generally a good way to filter out candidates who might get intimidated by your display of uninterest. After you successfully said everything you wanted, pass the word to them, and try to squeeze in a few yawns. Make sure you look away from the monitor, and try to count sheep while they talk, this technique helps with keeping up a regular rate of yawns. You might be surprised how efficient and rather addictive this method can be.

#### 6. Ask the right questions

There's nothing more important in the tech world then tech! It is important that you let the things what really matter sink in. Does their general problem solving and learning abilities matter? Does their portfolio and list of side projects matter?  Does their morale, their work ethic, their communication skills matter? Why bother poking into this ambiguous terms of delusional and unnecessary sophistication, when you have the magic formula handed to you on a silver spoon? Simply go back to step one, compare their profile to the list of your requirements, and you should have a great filter in place.

If you are a programmer interviewing other programmers, it might be difficult to grasp this concept in the beginning, since you might have memories of your career when you didn't know something and somebody trusted you to be capable enough to pick it up, but you have to set emotions aside and focus on your true purpose: isolate and simplify problems which pay off quickly, therefore make it easier to survive the day without getting unnecessarily overwhelmed. Remember: complexity is real only if you acknowledge it, you have to stick with quantifiable metrics, detach the noise in order to reach a state clairty.

You should also ask about their salary requirements for future reference in point #10.

#### 7. Assess their skillset

In short, there are several strategies to get this right, which I've experienced many companies doing these days. We've talked about these approaches during the planning, let's see a few examples, which are inspired by the most impressive real life interviews I took part in lately:

- Idea for a take home task: ask them to write you an app, while giving them only one or two pointers, and let them fill out the gaps. It's important to be as least specific here as you can, and make sure they can't ask questions before or during the task. Make sure the criteria vaguely describes your entire product. The formula to determine how much time you should estimate on this task is to ask your lead developer how long it would take, and divide the value by 50. This is heavily inspired by competitive market philosophy of capitalism: the pool of candidates will compete for the prize of a job, this works especially well in desperate times. Based on the results you will be able to determine who is the person who puts in that extra, so you can take advantage of them in the future any time you need to without them giving you shit about it.

- Idea for a live coding exercise: write down a few specific questions. You can use code examples here with some expected output which they must guess. Make sure this code looks like something you would never approve in your production apps, and pick something you've ideally never heard of. Good candidates for this are deprecated language features, or just using an old version of your fvorite library which tehy most likely won't remember. Cap the time they can spend on each question and make sure you prevent them from actually running the code. Best is to just screenshare a word file so they can't copy it. The answer should be easily findable by search, therefore you must prohibit them from using any help. This will ensure they can work undisruptedly even when the internet is down. If you memorize the internet, you don't need to search, right?

- Idea for a live coding exercise: think of complex feature. If you can't, it's totally fine, you can just base it off last sprint's tickets for the team, just shove all the criteria into one big text file. You can use codesandbox, where you can upload the output of a create-react-app. Schedule a 30min call with your candidates. Start with opening the page with the criteria, and then start casually talking about the company, your hobbies and random realizations you've made in the past couple of days, like how raspberries look like they are from another planet, and that you have seen a cloud yesterday which looked exactly like a head of a turtle attached to Lady Gaga's left elbow. Once you have built enough tension, you can lean back in satisfaction and watch them melt. You should make it clear that they can use any tool they want, so this way you can make sure they already have a ChatGPT subscription, and your company won't have to pay for it, because, to be fair at this point that is the only reasonable choice to turn to. They if they are having a good day and get a reasonable working result out of the AI. For the ones, who manage to miraculously make it in time, you will know that you can count on the 1000+ lines of code being delivered by them per every 30 minutes during their employment. You  nailed it!

#### 8. Have backup

The hiring process is a slippery slope, you can never trust anyone before they sign a contract. As people proove over and over that they are unreliable and elusive, you have to keep collecting as many of them as you can, to win this number game.

Keep your ad running, so new people can join the process anytime, until eventually somebody signs, maybe even after.

#### 9. Choose the right person

Last, but not least, let's talk about time management. As we've seen, hiring doesn't happen overnight, so it's important that you don't let the hiring process interfere with your daily work.

If your company has less resources, you can always opt for the old classic, ghosting. Ghosting has been getting a bit of a negative backlash. A bunch of bitter applicants are leading this movement, who would just refuse to read the signs, and apparently wait around for months, filled with hope that their application can lead somewhere, and then try to blaim it on you.

Fortunately, there is a modern and very well crafted solution for this. Any modern HR machinery has it, and you can set it up just in a few steps:

- ask ChatGPT to write you an email, or just use the following text:
<blockquote className="bg-muted ml-8 border-l-2 border-muted-foreground pl-4">
"Thank you for applying for the [Position Name] position at [Company Name]. We appreciate the time and effort you put into your application and the opportunity to review your qualifications.

After careful consideration, we have decided to move forward with other candidates whose experience and skills more closely align with our current needs. This decision was not an easy one, as we received many strong applications.

We wish you the best of luck in your job search and future endeavors. Thank you again for your interest in joining our team."
</blockquote>

- paste the content into your HR engine so that whenever you click the big red button next to the person's name which would trigger sending out the email

Make sure the email is sent from a no-reply email address, so they cannot harass you asking for more specific feedback. I mean, you did your part, HR is not charity. People need to man up and just get better instead of expecting you to do everything.

#### 10. Make an offer

Well, after you managed to put all the hard work in to narrow down the oversupplied pool of candidates, you should have one person at this stage who you want to hire.

In order to make the most out of this, and to prove your brilliance, you can utilise the preparatory work you did in step #6. As the market is flooded, you should definitely cut the offer and remove at least 10-20k from the initially agreed compensation. You can imagine if someone took all the effort to go through the process, they most likely won't bail now, as they'd need to restart somewhere else, and wait months to get into the same position, so it's simply makes no sense for them financially to refuse the offer, which is still a great deal!
